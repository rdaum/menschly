# Every port paid for its bubbles somehow

Each 8-bit and 16-bit Bubble Bobble port bought its crowd of bubbles by giving something up. The NES and Master System versions kept every moving object a hardware sprite and accepted flicker, with the Master System cycling its sprite table in documented steps of 7. The ZX Spectrum port is the best-documented engine of all. It drew everything in software in two tiers: up to nine "main" sprites (two players, seven enemies) were erased from a background-only back buffer and redrawn with masks, scheduled around an estimated raster position to avoid flicker. Bubbles took a cheaper path that blends each bubble with the background and overwrites the screen, accepting overlap errors and flicker. The Atari ST/Amiga also drew in software and accepted slowdown, apparently running at about 25 fps. The C64 mixed hardware sprites with character-based "software sprites" written into colour RAM. That much is documented from the C64 code, but no verified source says which objects (bubbles included) were drawn in characters. Period techniques fall into a few families: character soft sprites (with pre-shifted copies for smooth motion), masked software sprites with background restore, vertical multiplexing by raster interrupt, flicker by rotating sprite priority, moving static objects into the tilemap, and running some updates at reduced rates. The MEGA65's raster rewrite buffer (RRB) removes the most expensive of these chores, erasing and redrawing pixels, because the VIC-IV composites GOTOX overlays as it draws each line. Applied to your engine, the arithmetic (inference, not measurement) says raster time is not the first limit you will hit with 30–60 bubbles. The likely failures are the 24-slot cap on a crowded ceiling row, the CPU cost per changed actor (about 2,900 cycles each today), and a row terminator that does not follow the documented rule. The Spectrum's split points to the right MEGA65 shape: a premium path for the few actors that matter, a cheap and tolerant path for the many bubbles, and updates done in raster order. Apart from the Spectrum disassembly write-up, port-level evidence is weak: mostly reviewer observation or code-adjacent hints, each marked as such below.

## Each port chose one sacrifice: flicker, frame rate, or background

The table compresses the port evidence. "Documented" means a primary or code-level source says it. "Reported" means reviewers or players observed it. "Inferred" means it follows from hardware facts but nobody has confirmed it.

| Port | How moving objects were drawn | What gave under load | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| C64 (Software Creations, 1987) | Hardware sprites plus software sprites in character/colour RAM; 2×2 hardware sprites for big bonuses | Little documented; a fan fix for Secret Room flicker; lower bubble spawn rate than the arcade | Hybrid documented from code; which objects are soft sprites is unknown |
| NES/Famicom (Taito, 1987–88) | Almost certainly OAM sprites for bubbles, enemies and dragons | Flicker plus "a little slowdown" | Reported, inferred |
| Master System (Taito, 1988) | Hardware sprites for all moving objects; static event items in the tilemap | Flicker; sprite table cycled in steps of 7 | Emulator-debugger observation |
| ZX Spectrum (Mike Follin, 1987) | Two tiers. Up to 9 main sprites: erased from a background back buffer, then drawn masked, scheduled around an estimated raster. Bubbles: blended with the background and overwritten, with no raster check | "The odd bit of slowdown"; bubble flicker and bubble-on-bubble overwrites accepted | Documented from disassembly |
| Amstrad CPC (1987) | CPU-drawn, chunky low-resolution | "Sluggishness in places" | Reported |
| MSX2 (Taito, 1987) | V9938 hardware sprites | Sprite "tearing"; severe slowdown with two players | Reported |
| Atari ST / Amiga (David Broadhurst) | Built on the ST and moved across with few changes; implies CPU software sprites | About 25 fps, plus "minor framerate issues" | 25 fps implied by a remake's comparison |

Among the hardware-sprite ports, the **C64** has the best evidence, from Victor Widell's reverse engineering of the 6510 code. He writes that "the software sprites that use the color ram all update it when drawn anyway," and shows hardware sprites switched through `$d015` at level transitions ([geon](https://geon.github.io/programming/2025/10/07/bubble-bobble-c64-continue-bug-fix)). The bonus watermelon alone takes **four hardware sprites (2×2)**, half the VIC-II's eight ([geon](https://geon.github.io/programming/2025/07/24/bubble-bobble-c64-rng-emulator)). Bubbles drift along a **per-tile wind map, one direction byte per 8×8 cell** at `$8500` ([geon](https://geon.github.io/programming/2025/01/05/bubble-bobble-c64-wind)). A forum snippet, which could not be read in full, says C64 bubbles "are not animated anywhere near full frame rate and are part of the background graphics" ([Sleeping Elephant forum](https://www.sleepingelephant.com/~sleeping/ipw-web/bulletin/bb/viewtopic.php?p=121015)). The inferred picture is bubbles as character-cell soft sprites riding the wind grid, with hardware sprites kept for dragons, enemies and big items. It is plausible but unconfirmed. Zzap!64 noted that "bubbles always mass at the top of the screen" ([Zzap!64 #30](https://everygamegoing.com/larticle/Bubble-Bobble-000/28482)). The remaster's developer calls bubble spawn rate and speed something with "no easy fix" ([Dave's Retro Forge](https://daves-retro-forge.itch.io/bubble-bobble-c64-remastered)), which hints, speculatively, at a fixed object budget built into the engine.

The **NES** port followed the console approach. Reviewers say the flicker "can be attributed to the prevalence of bubbles and enemies, which is sometimes too much for the NES to handle" ([Cubed3](https://www.cubed3.com/games/reviews/nes/bubble-bobble)), and users report "a little slowdown when there is a lot of bubbles" ([GameFAQs](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/nes/563392-bubble-bobble/reviews/45075)). Background tiles never flicker on the NES, so flickering bubbles are very likely sprites (strong inference). The hardware allows **64 sprites, 8 per scanline** ([NESdev Wiki](https://nesdev.org/wiki/Sprite_size)). If each object is four 8×8 sprites, which a hobbyist bot's OAM addresses weakly suggest ([bubblebot](https://github.com/dps2004/bubblebot)), at most 16 objects fit in OAM per frame. Busier screens can only be shown by time-sharing.

The **Master System** port gives the one concrete rotation figure. Calindro, author of the Emulicious emulator, inspected the sprite table and reported "it seems to cycle them at steps of 7 sprites." He also confirmed that auto-spawned event items such as crowns sit in the tilemap, while "everything [else] seems subject to standard sprite disappearance" beyond 8 per line ([SMS Power!](https://www.smspower.org/forums/15251-BubbleBobbleBadGraphicalDevelopmentDecisions)). The developers chose the split deliberately. In the same thread, programmers warn that "writing to the name table a lot is relatively slow" for moving objects, and say that anything not moving continuously could become background, "better if grid-aligned" ([SMS Power!](https://www.smspower.org/forums/15251-BubbleBobbleBadGraphicalDevelopmentDecisions)). Reviewers disagree on the result, from "dozens of moving sprites with minimal flicker" ([Sega-16](https://www.sega-16.com/2026/11/final-bubble-bobble/)) to "a pretty major problem… given the massive amount of bubbles" ([Overclocked Sega Ages](https://overclockworked.neocities.org/segaages/bubble/)).

The machines without sprites had to draw every object with the CPU. The **Spectrum** is the one port whose renderer is documented from disassembly, in VilleKrum's emix8.org write-up. It is based on the shipped code; Kevin Edwards holds the original commented source, written on a Tatung Einstein ([emix8 bbdisasm, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20250613002940/https://www.emix8.org/bbdisasm/)). The facts below come from that write-up as relayed by the coordinator; the saved PDF of it was not available to this writer. Sprites are drawn **straight to the screen, with no double buffer**. A **background-only back buffer at 0xE000**, rebuilt for each level, is the source for erasing. The **main sprites, at most 9 (2 players + 7 enemies)**, are 16 rows tall, with **96 bytes per frame** (graphic plus mask, 3 bytes per row; the 24-pixel row width suggests a 16-pixel image shifted within it, an inference). They are erased by AND-masking from the back buffer and then drawn masked, by a routine at 0xA6F3. That routine is split into pre, mid and post sections so that the 8 pixel rows inside one character cell run as an unrolled `INC H` sequence. A **raster-aware scheduling loop (0xA3AD, `RASCHEK`)** never reads the raster. It advances an *estimated* beam position by fixed costs per action: erasing takes 7 lines aligned or 20 unaligned, painting takes 8 aligned or 11 unaligned. Any sprite whose old or new Y span falls inside a 30-line window around the estimate is skipped and drawn later. This avoids tearing without hardware help. **Bubbles use a separate, cheaper routine.** It blends the bubble with the back-buffer background and *overwrites* the screen, so a slow bubble needs no erase pass; only fast ones, such as just-blown or bursting bubbles, are erased. Bubbles do not blend with each other, so one simply overwrites another, and they skip the raster check, accepting flicker ([emix8, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20250613002940/https://www.emix8.org/bbdisasm/)). The design spends cycles on the few objects players watch and economises on the swarm. The rest of the evidence agrees with it: reviewers saw "see-through characters on colourful platforms on black backgrounds, with just the odd bit of slowdown when things get frantic" ([RetroArcadia](https://retroarcadia.blog/2024/08/21/my-life-with-bubble-bobble-on-spectrum-st-arcade-everywhere/)) and "no clash visible anywhere" ([CRASH 45](https://www.crashonline.org.uk/45/bubble.htm)). The **16-bit** versions were written by David Broadhurst on the ST first, then sent to the Amiga over a serial cable ([Broadhurst](https://www.davidbroadhurst.me/projects/bubble-bobble)). A later remake promised "50 fps instead of 25, 32 colours instead of 16" ([Indie Retro News](https://www.indieretronews.com/2020/08/tiny-bobble-arcade-quality-port-of.html)), which implies the original ran at half the display rate, a classic software-sprite budget. Nothing documented says the Amiga version used its blitter or hardware sprites. The **MSX2** version was Taito's own; it showed tearing when busy and "severe slowdown in two players mode" ([MSX Resource Center](https://www.msx.org/downloads/bobble-bubble-improvement-patches-v096); [The King of Grabs](https://thekingofgrabs.com/2021/11/04/bubble-bobble-msx/)).

No source gives a maximum bubble count for any port. Only the Spectrum's limit on main sprites (9) is documented. Whether ports refused new bubbles, popped old ones, or slowed down under overload is not documented anywhere. On the C64, no verified source says which objects were character soft sprites. A Martin Piper code-analysis video ([YouTube](https://youtu.be/O3eAtyBHOOc?t=855)) went unreviewed, so that question stays open.

## Seven period techniques, each a trade of memory, time or correctness

**Character soft sprites** place an object by writing character codes into screen memory. Moving one column on the Spectrum is "add one to the screen address… straightforward, but it is not smooth" ([oldmachinery](https://oldmachinery.blogspot.com/2014/04/zx-sprites.html)). Smooth motion requires **pre-shifting**: "each graphic needs 8 shifted variants", and a shifted 16×16 sprite becomes "in reality a 24x16 sprite", costing memory ([oldmachinery](https://oldmachinery.blogspot.com/2014/04/zx-sprites.html)). **Masked software sprites with background restore** AND out a mask and OR in the graphic. The same author's tuned Z80 routine managed only **eight 16×16 masked, smoothly moving sprites** per frame once buffer and clearing work were counted ([oldmachinery](https://oldmachinery.blogspot.com/2014/04/zx-sprites.html)). That ceiling explains why the Spectrum and ST ports slowed down rather than flickered. XOR drawing makes erasing free but breaks on overlaps and colour ([Ralph Becket](https://ralphbecket.blogspot.com/2015/09/faster-sprites-on-zx-spectrum.html)). Spectrum Bubble Bobble uses two different erase methods, neither of them XOR. Its main sprites restore the background by AND-mask from a clean back buffer. Its bubbles skip the erase altogether: the background is blended into each bubble image and stamped over the old one ([emix8, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20250613002940/https://www.emix8.org/bbdisasm/)). Its `RASCHEK` loop adds a seventh technique, **beam-chasing by estimate**: work is ordered so the CPU never writes where the beam is about to draw.

**Vertical multiplexing** reuses the C64's eight sprites further down the screen. A multiplexer sorts virtual sprites by Y and fires raster IRQs about 10–12 lines ahead, but "it is simply impossible to display more than 8 sprites on the same raster line" ([Cadaver](https://cadaver.github.io/rants/sprite.html)). Sorting is the main cost. Continuous insertion sort ("Ocean sort") exploits the fact that order rarely changes between frames ([Cadaver](https://cadaver.github.io/rants/sprite.html)). A field sort handles 32 actors in **at most 2,208 cycles** ([Linus Åkesson](https://hd0.linusakesson.net/programming/fieldsort/index.php)). **Flicker rotation** handles the per-line limit that remains. NES "OAM cycling" reorders sprites each frame so a line with more than 8 flickers instead of losing the same sprite permanently ([NESdev archive](https://archive.nes.science/nesdev-forums/f3/t5686.xhtml)). The Master System Bubble Bobble's step of 7 is one instance. Seven does not divide 64, so every sprite eventually passes through every priority position (inference).

**Objects becoming background** is documented for the Master System's crowns and recommended for anything "not moving continuously" ([SMS Power!](https://www.smspower.org/forums/15251-BubbleBobbleBadGraphicalDevelopmentDecisions)). No named period game was found that converts *bubbles* that have come to rest into tiles. **Reduced update rates** are documented mainly for C64 Blood Money. Its sprite update ran every 3 frames, later nearly 2, while the player moved every VBlank, which felt smoother than the overall rate ([Mike Dailly](https://lemmings.info/rasters-sprites-and-multiplexing/)). The ST/Amiga Bubble Bobble's apparent 25 fps is the same move applied to the whole game. Advice to stagger AI across frames comes mostly from modern forums ([Unity discussion](https://discussions.unity.com/t/managing-realtime-roguelike-timestep/654110)).

All seven techniques answer two questions: who pays for erasing, and who decides which objects lose when a line is full.

## The MEGA65 removes the redraw cost but keeps a per-line limit

The VIC-IV's RRB answers the first question in hardware. A GOTOX token "draws nothing" and moves the render position to a 10-bit X, letting characters overlay earlier ones on the same line. The chipset reference likens these overlays to Amiga bobs "with the advantage that they are rendered in the display pipeline, and thus do not need to be un-drawn and re-drawn" ([MEGA65 Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf)). In the GOTOX token's colour bytes, the 16-colour (NCM) row mask and the Y offset give vertical placement within a row "without needing to copy the character data" ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf); [Dan Sanderson](https://dansanderson.com/mega65/pixies/)). Pre-shifting and background save/restore become unnecessary. Your engine already uses both features.

The second question remains, in a different form. The official capacity statement is only "limited only by the raster time of the VIC-IV. Some experimentation will be required" ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf)). The best measured figure is Dan Sanderson's stress test: 12 rows of 20 NCM characters as full-width layers. **Real hardware handled 11 layers and missed lines at 12; Xemu handled 12** ([Sanderson, RRB article](https://dansanderson.com/mega65/raster-rewrite-buffer/)). That works out to roughly **3,500 overlay pixels per line** on stock hardware (inference by multiplication, and only for NCM). A third-party FPGA fork quotes per-token cycle costs that conflict with Sanderson's results, so they should not be used for stock planning ([beniacm](https://github.com/beniacm/mega65-k325t-cm4)). When the budget runs out, the line is cut off on the right. **DBLRR** ($D051.6) updates the buffer every second raster line, giving up vertical resolution (V200) for more RRB cycles ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf); [Sanderson](https://dansanderson.com/mega65/raster-rewrite-buffer/)). Real hardware and Xemu differ in edge cases, so test on hardware.

Two documented rules apply directly to you. First, **every row must end with a GOTOX to the right edge followed by a character**, because the VIC-IV only shows data up to and including the last character drawn ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf); [Sanderson](https://dansanderson.com/mega65/raster-rewrite-buffer/); [RetroCogs](https://retrocogs.mega65.com/2025/08/14/vic-iv-graphics-using-rrb-for-pixies/)). Your rows end with the GOTOX and no character after it. That breaks the documented rule. The notes do not establish what goes wrong; it may happen to work in Xemu or for some layouts. Appending one transparent character per row costs 2 screen bytes and 2 colour-RAM bytes, and removes the question. Second, Sanderson recommends padding unused row memory with dummy characters and clearing HOTREG ($D05D.7) ([Sanderson](https://dansanderson.com/mega65/raster-rewrite-buffer/)). The VIC-IV also has 8 hardware sprites with their own palette, plus a per-sprite ring buffer that "potentially" allows horizontal reuse ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf)).

## A 60-bubble design: slots and CPU bookkeeping run out before raster time

Everything in this section is inference from the documented figures above and from your engine's source (`lib/actors.mensch`, `scripts/bubble_bobble/bubble-game.mensch`). None of it is measured.

**Raster time has headroom.** A row today paints 40 background characters of 8 px (320 px) and 24 slots of one 16-pixel NCM character each (384 px), about 700 px plus 25 GOTOX tokens. That is roughly a fifth of Sanderson's ~3,500 px hardware result. Even 32 slots (the maximum `actors_setup` accepts) comes to about 830 px of overlay. Three caveats apply. GOTOX cost on stock hardware is undocumented. Empty slots, which draw a character at x=320, probably still consume paint time. Background characters in a full-colour mode cost more per pixel than NCM. None of these is likely to eat a 4–5× margin, so DBLRR is a reserve lever, not a first step. One GOTOX-heavy benchmark on real hardware (for example 32 slots all at x<320 on one row) would turn this estimate into a fact.

**Copy the Spectrum's two tiers.** Spectrum Bubble Bobble ran its expensive, correct path for at most 9 actors, and a cheap path that tolerates errors for the swarm ([emix8, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/20250613002940/https://www.emix8.org/bbdisasm/)). The MEGA65 equivalent has three parts.

1. **Premium tier: dragons and enemies.** Put them on hardware sprites where possible. Any that spill into software actors get guaranteed slots and update every frame.
2. **Bubble tier: cheaper and allowed to be imperfect.**
   - Bubbles share a handful of pool cells, so a bubble whose cell did not change never needs a pool or palette lookup.
   - A bubble can update every second frame.
   - When a row is full, a bubble can lose its slot and flicker.
   - The order of overlapping bubbles may be wrong. The Spectrum shipped with bubbles overwriting each other.
3. **Update in raster order.** The Spectrum's `RASCHEK` estimated the beam position and skipped any sprite near it. On the MEGA65 the raster position can be read directly, so the inferred equivalent is simpler. Sort dirty actors by first text row (the RRB already groups slots by row), and process them top to bottom starting in the bottom border, so each row is rewritten before the beam reaches it. Then `actors_frame` no longer has to fit inside the border. The text area is 26 rows × 8 = 208 lines of a 312-line PAL frame, which leaves about 100 lines outside it (inference). Any row the CPU cannot reach in time is deferred to the next frame, not torn.

**Slots per row are the real cap, and they fail badly.** A 16×16 bubble occupies 2 text rows when aligned to the 8-pixel grid and 3 when not. Sixty bubbles use about 150 row-slots in total, against 26 × 24 = 624 available, so the total is fine. The trouble is that bubbles do not spread evenly: Bubble Bobble's defining behaviour is that they "mass at the top of the screen" ([Zzap!64](https://everygamegoing.com/larticle/Bubble-Bobble-000/28482)). The room is 256 px wide, or 16 bubbles abreast. A clump two bubbles deep at mixed sub-row offsets can put 24–32 bubbles across one text row, before counting overlap. When a row is full, the engine leaves that part of the actor out "until the actor next moves." For a bubble resting at the ceiling, that means a permanently sliced bubble, the worst failure among the period options. Three fixes follow from the period techniques. Raise `SLOTS` towards 32, which costs memory, not raster time. Let a row-full event mark the actor for retry every frame, or rotate which actors win contested rows, Master System style, so overflow shows as flicker rather than a stuck slice. Rank allocation so dragons, enemies inside bubbles and fruit claim slots before empty bubbles do. Slot order is draw order, so rotation also reorders overlapping bubbles, which matters little when they are identical.

**CPU cost per changed actor is the lever worth pulling.** One PAL logical raster is 63 cycles at ~1 MHz ([Chipset Reference](https://files.mega65.org/files/m/mega65-chipset-reference_cnFcKB.pdf)), about 2,550 cycles at 40.5 MHz. Your 25 lines for ~22 changed actors is about 64,000 cycles, or **~2,900 cycles per actor**, to rewrite 2–3 slots of 4 bytes each. Extrapolated linearly, 60 changed actors would take ~68 lines, about 22% of a 312-line frame, before any game logic. Most of that per-actor cost is overhead, not byte writes. Four changes follow, in order of expected payoff:

1. **Rewrite only what changed.** Add a fast path for an actor that stays in the same rows with the same cell: it needs only the GOTOX X bytes and the Y shift/mask bytes rewritten, with no slot search and no pool or palette lookup.
2. **Keep positions in fixed point and mark an actor dirty only when its integer pixel changes.** Slow-drifting bubbles then become dirty every second or third frame without any explicit scheduling.
3. **Stagger what can wait.** Run bubble wall, ceiling and bubble-to-bubble collision on a quarter of the bubbles each frame (index mod 4), the Blood Money pattern, while the dragon updates every frame ([Mike Dailly](https://lemmings.info/rasters-sprites-and-multiplexing/)).
4. **Bucket bubbles by text row.** The RRB already forces this grouping, and it doubles as the broad phase that keeps bubble-to-bubble pushing from growing as O(N²).

DMA jobs are poor value here. Each slot write is only a few scattered bytes, so job setup would cost more than the copy. Keep DMA for loading characters and images into the pool.

**Baking resting bubbles into the background buys little on the MEGA65.** On the Master System, moving static objects into the tilemap saved both sprite slots and per-frame work. In your engine an unchanged actor already costs no CPU, so the RRB gives the work saving for free. Baking would free only slots, and it forces grid alignment and gives up the arcade's ceiling wobble. Use it as a last resort for overflowing rows, not as the main design.

**Hardware sprites fit the actors that must never drop out.** Use the 8 hardware sprites for Bub, Bob and the most important enemies. The C64 port appears to have made the same split, though which objects it put on hardware sprites is unconfirmed. That keeps them out of slot competition entirely. With two players plus up to about seven enemies, a few enemies still need software slots, and they should rank first in allocation.

## Conclusion

The period ports show that no 1987 machine displayed an arcade-sized bubble swarm without visibly giving something up. The interesting variable was which compromise the designers chose, not whether they made one. Consoles chose flicker because their hardware sprites made pixels free and lines scarce. Computers without sprites chose a lower frame rate because pixels were what cost them. The MEGA65's RRB makes pixels nearly free and makes a line's capacity a matter of configuration. The compromise left over is bookkeeping: how many slots a row has, who wins a full row, and how many cycles an update costs.

For your engine, the next steps are concrete and cheap. Terminate each row with a character after the final GOTOX. Benchmark one row with every slot in use on real hardware to confirm the raster headroom. Then follow the Spectrum's lead rather than baking bubbles into the background: give the swarm its own cheap, imperfect path; make dropped slots flicker rather than stick; and update rows in beam order. On this evidence, 60 bubbles is a bookkeeping problem the existing architecture can absorb, not a hardware limit. That conclusion rests on inference until the benchmark runs.
